# Agent Airlock short film

Producer notes to the director and the writer.

You own the company, the people, the night, the camera, the dialogue, and the plot. I own the audience. This file is what I will feel from the seat, and what I will not accept.

Deliver one self-contained animated film, about six minutes, 1920x1080, H.264 plus AAC, captions on.

When the lights come up I want one sentence left in the room: the organization sets what agents may do, and Airlock makes that binding.

Until then, I am not here for a product. I am here for these people.

## From the audience chair

I do not know this product. I will not read a repo. I will not stay for a clever explanation.

I stay because I care what happens to someone on screen.

By the first minutes I need a person I would recognize at work: tired, competent, trying to do right under a clock. I need to know what they want tonight, who is waiting on them, and what it will cost if they fail. If I do not feel that, I am gone.

Do not make me admire them from a distance. Put me in their chair. Let me feel the prompt as a reasonable thing I might type. Let me hope with them. Then make me afraid for them.

When the blast radius hits, I should not think "interesting risk." I should think of that person. The pager, the customer, the teammate who tried to help, the lead asking who allowed this — those land because I already care who is going to have to answer.

If I can describe the plot but not the people, you failed. If I forget the product name but remember a face in a quiet room after the damage, you are close. If I remember the face and then understand why Airlock had to exist for them, you are done.

Give me several people, not a hero and a chorus. Let them want different things. Nobody is a cartoon villain. The trouble starts from a prompt that is plausible and underspecified, not from a fool.

## What I am buying

Teams are putting agents on work that can write, publish, and change real systems. A prompt is not a policy. A hurried engineer is not a change-control board. A model is not the owner of the company.

I want to feel that gap in my body. Then I want to feel the difference when the team owns the rules and those rules sit in the path of action.

Agent Airlock is the checkpoint: intent on one side, supported action on the other, a verdict the organization can stand behind.

This is not a feature tour. This is not a sermon about AI. This is that job, told so I cannot look away.

## Demands to the writer

1. Make me love someone before you break something. Stake first. Argument second.
2. Write people, not roles. I should be able to care about them if you stripped the job titles.
3. Cover more than one way an agent can overreach. Each one must cost a human being something I can feel.
4. Play outages and repercussions as scenes, not as news. Who gets paged. Who faces the customer. Who has to say they did not mean it. What dies downstream. Make it expensive.
5. Governance is the spine, not a closing card. The same rules bind the team, not one chat. Policy lives outside the prompt and outside the agent's writable paths. Urgency, helpful teammates, `/yolo`, auto-approve, and retries cannot widen rights. Local work can still move. Forbidden outbound and sensitive actions cannot slip through. When someone later asks what happened, the answer is a record, not a shrug.
6. Airlock is the turn, not the lecture. After I understand the cost of missing org control, show me the same kinds of request with the team in charge. Contrast is the argument. If you have to explain the point, the scene did not work.
7. End on people who can work again, not on a slogan.

## Demands to the director

1. This must play as a movie. Continuous motion. Character. Tension. Consequence. I refuse slides, title cards with fade, and talking heads with bullets.
2. I want exhilarating animation: kinetic frames, bodies in rooms, light that changes with the night, interfaces that feel alive, cameras that hunt and hold. If a shot could be a still graphic with a voiceover, cut it.
3. I want dramatic scenes I will remember with my stomach. Peaks. Near-misses. The moment a command is about to land. The silence after. Do not montage the pain. Stay in it long enough that I cannot intellectualize it.
4. Put spectacle in service of the person I already care about. A cascading failure is only as good as the face watching it. An Airlock block is only as good as the relief, frustration, or argument in the room.
5. Sound and picture should do work the narration does not. Prefer a look, a pause, a slammed laptop, a held breath. If you narrate, it must sound like a short film, not a demo.
6. Keep me in the story world. Never mention a hack, a demo, a proof of concept, a science fair, a sample repo, or that no production outage occurred. No disclaimers. No wink.
